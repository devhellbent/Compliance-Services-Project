Secretarial Audit

OVERVIEW

In 2026, a Secretarial Audit is a mandatory, independent compliance audit conducted by a Practicing Company Secretary (PCS). It ensures that a company adheres to the Companies Act, 2013, SEBI regulations, FEMA, and other applicable corporate laws.

The audit acts as a "legal health check," verifying non-financial records like board minutes, share transfers, and statutory filings. It mitigates legal risks, protects directors from liability, and enhances stakeholder confidence by providing an objective assurance of the company’s governance and regulatory integrity.

Benefits of Company Secretarial Audit

In 2026, a Secretarial Audit is considered the "non-financial counterpart" to a statutory financial audit. While a financial audit checks the numbers, the Secretarial Audit ensures that the company’s legal and governance architecture is sound.

The benefits of this audit extend to various stakeholders, acting as a multi-pronged tool for risk management and brand building.

Protection for Directors and Management

The "Officers in Default" (Directors and KMPs) are personally liable for compliance failures under the Companies Act.

Evidence of Due Diligence: A Secretarial Audit report acts as legal proof that the Board took proactive steps to comply with the law, which can limit personal liability in case of technical lapses.

Early Warning System: It identifies procedural gaps—like improper board minutes or delayed filings—before they result in heavy penalties or disqualification of directors.

Strategic and Financial Advantages

Enhanced Investor Confidence: Institutional investors and PE firms in 2026 view a "Clean" Secretarial Audit Report (Form MR-3) as a certificate of high ethical standards, often leading to better valuations during funding rounds.

Smoother M&A and Fundraising: During mergers or IPOs, having a history of secretarial audits significantly reduces the time and cost of the "Legal Due Diligence" process.

Borrowing Power: Banks and financial institutions are more likely to offer favorable interest rates to companies that demonstrate a robust "Compliance Management System" through regular audits.

Operational and Cultural Benefits

Institutionalized Discipline: It forces the company to maintain its statutory registers and records in real-time, preventing the "last-minute panic" before annual filings.

Employee and Vendor Trust: For companies with high ESG (Environmental, Social, and Governance) scores, a secretarial audit proves they are "Good Corporate Citizens," which helps in attracting top-tier talent and reliable global partners.

Risk Mitigation: It covers not just the Companies Act, but also FEMA (for foreign investments) and Industry-Specific Laws, ensuring that the company doesn't accidentally violate complex cross-border regulations.

Eligible Criteria for Company Secretarial Audit

In 2026, the eligibility (applicability) for a Secretarial Audit is governed by Section 204 of the Companies Act, 2013, read with Rule 9 of the Companies (Appointment and Remuneration of Managerial Personnel) Rules, 2014.

The audit is not optional for companies that meet specific size, nature, or borrowing thresholds.

Mandatory Thresholds (2026)

A company must conduct a Secretarial Audit if it falls into any of the following categories:

Listed Companies: Every company whose securities are listed on any recognized stock exchange in India.

Public Companies (Unlisted):

Paid-up Share Capital: ₹50 Crore or more.

Turnover: ₹250 Crore or more.

Companies with High Borrowings: * Any company (including Private Companies) having outstanding loans or borrowings from banks or public financial institutions of ₹100 Crore or more.

Material Unlisted Subsidiaries: Under SEBI (LODR) Regulations, any unlisted material Indian subsidiary of a listed entity must also undergo this audit.

Specific 2026 Eligibility Nuances

Deemed Public Companies: A private company that is a subsidiary of a public company is treated as a "public company" for this Act. If the parent or the subsidiary meets the thresholds above, the audit becomes mandatory for the subsidiary as well.

"Point in Time" Borrowings: For the ₹100 Crore borrowing limit, the amount is checked as it stood on the last date of the latest audited financial statement.

Materiality Threshold: For subsidiaries, "material" generally means the subsidiary's income or net worth exceeds 10% of the consolidated income or net worth of the listed parent company.

Documents Required for Company Secretarial Audit

In 2026, a Secretarial Audit requires a vast array of records to prove that a company is compliant with the Companies Act, SEBI norms, FEMA, and other industry-specific laws. The auditor (a Practicing Company Secretary) will inspect these documents to issue the final report in Form MR-3.

The required documents are generally categorized into five primary buckets:

Charter & Constitutional Documents

These are the foundational "legal identity" documents of the company.

Certificate of Incorporation: Original and any amended certificates (in case of name changes).

Memorandum & Articles of Association (MoA & AoA): Duly updated with all amendments made to date.

Commencement of Business Certificate: If applicable for older companies.

Statutory Registers (The "Core" Records)

Under the Companies Act 2013, maintaining these is non-negotiable. The auditor will check for physical or digital signatures and timely updates.

Register of Members (MGT-1): Details of shareholders and their holdings.

Register of Directors & KMP (MGT-10): Particulars of directors, CEO, CFO, and CS.

Register of Charges (CHG-7): Records of loans secured against company assets.

Register of Loans & Investments (MBP-2): Details of inter-corporate loans and investments (Section 186).

Register of Related Party Transactions (MBP-4): Contracts where directors have an interest.

Register of Significant Beneficial Owners (SBO): Required to track ultimate ownership.

Board & Meeting Documentation

This is the most scrutinized section, as it proves the company's decision-making integrity.

Notice, Agenda, and Notes on Agenda: Proof that they were sent to directors at least 7 days in advance (SS-1 compliance).

Attendance Registers: Signed sheets for Board, Committee, and General Meetings.

Minute Books: Signed and dated minutes of all Board, Audit Committee, NRC, and General Meetings (AGM/EGM).

Postal Ballot Records: Notices, scrutinizer reports, and results of any resolutions passed via postal ballot.

Filings & Financial Disclosures

Annual Filings: Copies of Form MGT-7 (Annual Return) and Form AOC-4 (Financial Statements).

Event-Based Forms: All forms filed with the ROC during the year (e.g., PAS-3 for allotments, DIR-12 for director changes, MGT-14 for special resolutions).

Directors' Disclosures: Form MBP-1 (Disclosure of Interest) and Form DIR-8 (Declaration of Non-disqualification) obtained annually from every director.

Audited Financial Statements: Including the Directors’ Report and Statutory Auditor’s Report.

Regulatory & Sector-Specific Compliance

Depending on the company's nature, the following are also checked:

SEBI Compliance (For Listed Firms): Quarterly filings, Insider Trading disclosures, and Shareholding Patterns.

FEMA Compliance: Form FC-GPR or FC-TRS for foreign investments and the Annual FLA Return.

Labor & Environmental Laws: PF/ESI challans, pollution control certificates, and factory licenses.

POSH Compliance: The Annual POSH Report and proof of Internal Committee (IC) meetings.

Legal Due Diligence

OVERVIEW

In 2026, Legal Due Diligence (LDD) is a comprehensive investigation into a company’s legal health, typically conducted before a merger, acquisition, or major investment. It involves a systematic review of corporate records, material contracts, intellectual property, and pending litigation to uncover hidden liabilities. By verifying the truth of a seller’s claims, LDD ensures the buyer is fully aware of any legal risks that could impact the deal's value or future operations.

This process is vital for informed decision-making and risk mitigation. It allows the acquiring party to negotiate better terms, adjust the purchase price, or request specific indemnities based on identified "red flags." Beyond standard compliance, modern LDD in 2026 also assesses critical areas like data privacy under the DPDPA and environmental sustainability, ensuring the transaction aligns with both current regulatory standards and long-term strategic goals.

Sections of a Due Diligence Report

In 2026, a Legal Due Diligence (LDD) Report is structured to provide investors or acquirers with a clear roadmap of risks. While the "Detailed Findings" can span hundreds of pages, the report follows a standardized hierarchy to ensure the most critical information is seen first.

The report is typically divided into the following key sections:

The "Front-End" (Executive View)

Cover Page: Includes the target company's name, Corporate Identification Number (CIN), date, and a strict confidentiality notice.

Executive Summary: The "Bottom Line." It summarizes the deal's viability and lists the top 3–5 risks that could potentially break the deal or require a price adjustment.

Risk Matrix (Red Flag Table): A color-coded table (Red, Amber, Green) that categorizes issues by severity, impact, and whether they must be fixed before (Pre-Closing) or after (Post-Closing) the deal is signed.

General Company Profile

Corporate Identity: Verification of incorporation date, registered office, and validity of the Memorandum and Articles of Association (MoA & AoA).

Capital Structure (The Cap Table): A detailed review of authorized vs. issued capital, shareholding patterns, and any dilutive instruments like ESOPs, convertibles, or warrants.

Detailed Subject Modules

This is the "Deep Dive" section where the legal team reviews specific areas of the business:

Material Contracts: Analysis of major vendor, customer, and lease agreements, specifically flagging "Change of Control" clauses.

Intellectual Property (IP): Verification of trademarks, patents, and software ownership. In 2026, this also includes an audit of Open Source license compliance.

Litigation & Disputes: A summary of all civil, criminal, and tax cases, including an assessment of potential financial payouts.

Employment & Labor: Audit of employment contracts, PF/ESI dues, and any history of POSH (Prevention of Sexual Harassment) complaints.

Regulatory & Data Privacy: Assessment of industry-specific licenses and compliance with the DPDP Act (Digital Personal Data Protection Act).

The "Back-End" (Supporting Evidence)

Scope & Limitations: Defines exactly what was reviewed (e.g., "records from the last 5 years only") and what documents were missing from the Data Room.

Assumptions: Standard legal assumptions (e.g., assuming all signatures provided are genuine).

Annexures: Copies of the most critical documents discovered, such as a major lawsuit filing or a "hidden" debt agreement.

Benefits of Legal Due Diligence

In 2026, Legal Due Diligence (LDD) has evolved from a simple "checklist" to a strategic "deal-shaper." Whether you are an investor looking at a startup or a large corporation eyeing a merger, the benefits of LDD impact the transaction's success long after the papers are signed.

The key benefits of Legal Due Diligence include:

Risk Mitigation and "Skeletal" Discovery

The most immediate benefit is the identification of "red flags"—hidden legal liabilities that the seller may not have disclosed or realized.

Litigation Shield: Uncovers pending or "threatened" lawsuits that could result in massive payouts post-acquisition.

Compliance Gaps: Identifies failures in statutory filings (ROC, GST) or labor law violations (PF, ESI) which, in 2026, carry much heavier penalties.

Legacy Liabilities: Detects long-term risks like environmental violations or unresolved tax disputes from previous decades.

Precise Valuation and Pricing Power

LDD findings are the primary weapon for price negotiation. If an auditor finds significant legal risks, the buyer has "hard facts" to:

Reduce the Purchase Price: Offset the cost of potential future legal battles against the current valuation.

Create Escrows: Keep a portion of the payment in a "blocked account" until specific legal issues (like a pending trademark dispute) are resolved.

Indemnification: Force the seller to promise (warrant) that they will pay for any legal losses arising from past actions.

In the digital economy of 2026, a company is only as valuable as its assets. LDD ensures those assets are real.

IP Chain of Title: Verifies that patents, trademarks, and software code are actually owned by the company and not by the individual founders or external contractors.

Clean Title to Property: For asset-heavy industries, it confirms that the land or buildings are free of "encumbrances" (undisclosed loans or mortgages).

Strategic and Integration Planning

LDD is forward-looking. It helps the new management plan for the "Day After" the deal.

Change of Control Risks: Identifies key customer or vendor contracts that might automatically terminate when the company changes hands.

Employee Retention: Reviews "Golden Parachutes" or restrictive non-compete clauses that might cause top talent to leave after the merger.

Cultural & Governance Fit: Assesses if the target's internal policies (like POSH or Data Privacy) align with the buyer's global standards.

Stakeholder and Investor Confidence

A clean LDD report serves as a "Certificate of Health" for external stakeholders.

Board Approval: Gives the Board of Directors the confidence to vote "Yes" on a deal, knowing they have exercised their fiduciary duty of care.

Lender Trust: Banks are more likely to fund an acquisition if a reputed law firm has vetted the target's legal standing.

Corporate Governance

OVERVIEW

In 2026, Corporate Governance is the overarching framework of rules, relationships, and processes by which a company is directed and controlled. It is essentially the "operating system" for ethical leadership, ensuring that a company is managed in a way that balances the interests of its many stakeholders.

While management handles the day-to-day operations, Governance is the responsibility of the Board of Directors to ensure those operations align with the long-term interests of the owners (shareholders) and society.

Pillars of Corporate Governance

In 2026, the Pillars of Corporate Governance are the fundamental principles that ensure a company is managed ethically, sustainably, and in the best interests of its stakeholders. While different frameworks may emphasize specific values, the globally recognized "Big Four" pillars form the bedrock of any robust governance system.

Accountability

Accountability is the obligation of the Board of Directors and Management to explain and take ownership of their actions and decisions.

Clarity of Roles: Ensuring that everyone—from the CEO to the Independent Directors—understands their specific mandate.

Answerability: Being prepared to justify strategic choices and financial results to shareholders during an Annual General Meeting (AGM).

Consequences: Establishing clear metrics for performance and ensuring that failure to meet ethical or legal standards results in corrective action.

Transparency

Transparency means being open and honest about the company’s operations, financials, and risks.

Full Disclosure: Providing timely and accurate information regarding profits, losses, and potential conflicts of interest.

Accessibility: Ensuring that reports (like the Annual Report or ESG disclosures) are easy to find and understand for even the smallest investor.

No Hidden Agendas: Operating in a way that "what you see is what you get," preventing the "asymmetry of information" that leads to corporate scandals.

Fairness

Fairness ensures that all stakeholders—regardless of their size or influence—receive equitable treatment.

Minority Rights: Protecting small shareholders from being "squeezed out" or ignored by large promoters.

Equal Treatment: Providing the same information and voting rights to all investors within the same class of shares.

Stakeholder Equity: Extending fairness beyond shareholders to include fair wages for employees and prompt payments to vendors.

Responsibility

Responsibility refers to the company’s duty to act as a "good corporate citizen."

ESG & Sustainability: In 2026, this pillar is heavily focused on Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) targets, ensuring the company doesn't sacrifice the planet for profit.

Legal Compliance: Strictly adhering to the laws of the land, such as the Companies Act or Data Privacy laws.

Ethical Leadership: Going beyond what is "legal" to do what is "right," fostering a culture of integrity throughout the organization.

OVERVIEW

In 2026, Internal Audit serves as an independent "health check" for an organization, focusing on risk management and compliance. It acts as a protective shield, ensuring the company adheres to legal standards, such as India’s DPDPA, while preventing fraud.

Process Review, conversely, acts as an efficiency engine. It scrutinizes workflows to identify bottlenecks and wastage. While the audit ensures you are "doing things right," the review ensures you are "doing the right things" optimally.

Objective of Internal Audit & Process Review

In 2026, the objectives of Internal Audit and Process Review have shifted from mere "policing" to becoming strategic partners in business growth. While their goals often overlap, they target two different aspects of organizational health.

Primary Objectives of Internal Audit

The core mission of an internal audit is to provide assurance and protection.

Evaluating Internal Controls: To verify that the checks and balances (like dual approvals for payments) are actually working to prevent errors or theft.

Risk Mitigation: To proactively identify emerging threats, such as cybersecurity vulnerabilities, regulatory changes, or ESG (Environmental, Social, and Governance) non-compliance.

Ensuring Statutory Compliance: To guarantee the company is adhering to laws like the Companies Act, FEMA, and the DPDP Act (Data Protection).

Safeguarding Assets: To ensure that both physical assets (inventory, property) and digital assets (data, IP) are protected from unauthorized access or misuse.

Primary Objectives of Process Review

The core mission of a process review is optimization and value creation.

Identifying Inefficiencies: To spot "bottlenecks" where tasks get stuck or redundant steps that add no value to the final product or service.

Standardization: To ensure that a process is performed the same way every time, regardless of who is doing it, leading to consistent quality.

Cost Reduction: To eliminate wastage of time, human effort, and financial resources by "leaning out" the workflow.

Alignment with Strategy: To ensure that the way a department operates actually supports the company's 2026 goals (e.g., ensuring a "Fast Delivery" promise is supported by an agile warehouse process).

Data Integrity: Decisions are only as good as the data they are based on. Audits verify that the reports reaching the CEO’s desk are accurate and not "sugar-coated."

Core Area of Internal Audit & Process Review

In 2026, the core areas of Internal Audit & Process Review encompass more than just financial spreadsheets. They cover every "nerve center" of a modern organization, ensuring that both the regulatory safety (Audit) and operational speed (Process) are optimized.

Here are the primary pillars where these functions focus their efforts:

Finance and Accounts (The Foundation)

This remains the most traditional area but has evolved with AI-driven detection.

Procure-to-Pay (P2P): Auditing the entire journey from requesting a vendor to final payment, ensuring no "ghost vendors" or duplicate invoices exist.

Order-to-Cash (O2C): Reviewing the sales cycle to ensure revenue is recognized correctly and credit limits are not being bypassed.

Treasury & Cash Management: Verifying that the company’s liquidity is safe and that dual-authorization is strictly enforced for all transfers.

Regulatory & Statutory Compliance

In 2026, a major core area is ensuring the company stays out of legal trouble.

Tax Compliance: Reviewing GST filings, TDS deductions, and income tax provisions to avoid heavy interest penalties.

Data Privacy (DPDPA): A critical new core area focused on how the company collects, stores, and deletes personal data of customers and employees.

Labor Law Compliance: Auditing PF, ESI, and Gratuity payments to ensure workers' rights are protected.

Human Resources and Payroll

Internal audits often find the most "leakage" in people-related processes.

Onboarding & Exit: Reviewing if background checks are actually done and if "Full & Final" settlements are calculated accurately.

Payroll Processing: Checking for "ghost employees" and ensuring that bonuses or overtime pay align with approved policies.

POSH & Ethics: Auditing the functionality of the Internal Committee (IC) and the whistle-blower mechanism.

Supply Chain and Operations

This is where Process Review adds the most value by finding hidden costs.

Inventory Management: Conducting physical "wall-to-wall" counts and comparing them against digital records to find shrinkage or theft.

Logistics & Freight: Reviewing shipping routes and vendor contracts to find more cost-effective ways to move goods.

Quality Control: Ensuring that the production process meets the set standards to avoid expensive product recalls.

Information Technology (IT) & Security

As businesses become fully digital, IT audit is no longer optional.

User Access Management: Ensuring that an employee who left the company six months ago doesn't still have access to the main server.

Vulnerability Assessment: Identifying "weak spots" in the software that could be targets for hackers.

Business Continuity: Checking if the "Back-up" servers actually work in case the main office loses power or data.

Documents for Internal Audit & Process Review

In 2026, the document request list for an Internal Audit & Process Review is more comprehensive than a standard financial audit. Because it examines both "what happened" (Audit) and "how it happened" (Process), auditors require access to both data and the underlying logic of your operations.

The required documents are typically organized into these five core folders:

The Governance & Framework Folder

These documents establish the "rules of the game" that the auditor will measure you against.

Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs): Written manuals for every department (Finance, HR, Sales).

Organization Chart: To verify "Segregation of Duties" and reporting lines.

Policy Documents: Code of Conduct, IT Security Policy, Travel & Expense Policy, and POSH Policy.

Previous Audit Reports: To check if past "Red Flags" were actually fixed.

Finance & Transactional Data

Used to trace money and detect "leakage" or unauthorized spending.

General Ledger (GL) Extracts: Raw data from your ERP (SAP, Oracle, Tally) for the audit period.

Accounts Payable (AP): Invoices, Purchase Orders (POs), and "Three-way Match" records (PO + GRN + Invoice).

Accounts Receivable (AR): Sales invoices, credit notes, and aging reports of customers who haven't paid.

Fixed Asset Register: A list of all company-owned laptops, machinery, and vehicles.

Human Resources & Payroll

Auditors look for "Ghost Employees" and compliance with labor laws.

Employee Master File: A list of all current and exited employees.

Payroll Registers: Monthly salary sheets including bonuses, deductions, and overtime.

Statutory Filings: Proof of payment for PF, ESI, and Professional Tax (PT).

Attendance & Leave Records: To ensure salary paid matches the time worked.

IT, Data, and Security

Critical for ensuring the "Process" isn't vulnerable to hacking or data loss.

User Access Logs: A list showing who has "Admin" access to your financial software.

Backup Logs: Evidence that data is being backed up daily and stored safely.

Software Licenses: Proof that all tools being used are legally owned.

Data Privacy Artifacts: Consent forms and data processing logs required under the DPDP Act.

Specialized Process Documents

Specific to the "Review" part of the engagement to find bottlenecks.

Material Contracts: Agreements with the top 10 vendors and clients.

Inventory Logs: Stock movement records (Inward/Outward) and latest physical count reports.

MIS Reports: The internal dashboards management uses to track performance (KPIs).

Exception Reports: Records of any "out-of-turn" approvals given by the CEO or Managers.
