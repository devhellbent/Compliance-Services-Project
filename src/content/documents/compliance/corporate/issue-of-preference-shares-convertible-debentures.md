Issue of Preference Shares / Convertible Debentures

OVERVIEW

Issuing Preference Shares allows a company to raise equity while offering investors a "preferred" status. These shareholders receive fixed dividends before equity holders and have priority during liquidation. However, they typically lack voting rights, making this an ideal tool for companies wanting capital without diluting management control.

Convertible Debentures are debt instruments that pay regular interest initially but offer the option to convert into equity shares after a specific period. This "hybrid" approach provides the company with cheaper debt today while offering investors the potential for future capital gains if the company's stock price rises.

Issue of Preference Shares

Preference shares are often called "hybrid" securities because they behave like a mix of a stock and a bond.

Fixed Dividend: Unlike equity shareholders, preference shareholders receive a fixed dividend (e.g., a "9% Preference Share").

Priority Status: They have a "preferential right" to be paid dividends before any money goes to common equity holders. If the company goes bankrupt, they are also paid back before equity holders.

No Voting Rights: In most cases, they do not get to vote on company matters, allowing the founders to raise money without losing control.

Types: They can be Cumulative (unpaid dividends carry forward), Participating (get extra profits), or Redeemable (repaid after a set period).

Issue of Convertible Debentures

A convertible debenture is a type of loan (debt) that can be changed into equity shares after a specific period.

Interest First: Initially, the investor acts as a lender and receives regular interest payments (coupon).

The "Conversion" Option: At a pre-decided time, the investor has the right to convert their debt into company shares, usually at a discounted price.

Lower Interest Rate: Because they offer the potential for future stock gains, these debentures usually carry a lower interest rate than traditional loans.

Delayed Dilution: For the company, this is "delayed equity." They get the cash now but don't have to issue new shares (diluting ownership) until much later.

Benefits of the Issue of Preference Shares / Convertible Debentures

Both Preference Shares and Convertible Debentures offer unique strategic advantages, serving as flexible tools for companies to manage their capital structure while appealing to different types of investors.

Benefits of Issuing Preference Shares

For a company, preference shares act as a "middle ground" between debt and equity:

No Dilution of Control: Since preference shareholders typically do not have voting rights, the existing management or founders can raise significant capital without losing their decision-making power.

No Fixed Legal Obligation: Unlike debt, if a company makes a loss, it is generally not legally bound to pay preference dividends (unless they are "cumulative"), providing a safety net during lean years.

Improved Borrowing Capacity: Because preference shares are considered part of the company's net worth (equity), they strengthen the balance sheet, making it easier to secure loans from banks later.

Appeal to Conservative Investors: They attract investors who want higher returns than a bank deposit but lower risk than regular equity.

Benefits of Issuing Convertible Debentures

Convertible debentures are often used by growing companies and startups to bridge the gap between debt and future equity:

Lower Interest Rates: Because investors get the "sweetener" of being able to convert their debt into stock later, they are usually willing to accept a lower interest rate than they would for a standard loan.

Tax Efficiency: The interest paid on debentures is a tax-deductible expense. This reduces the company’s overall tax liability, which is not the case with dividends.

Delayed Equity Dilution: The company gets the cash immediately but doesn't have to issue new shares (diluting current owners) until the conversion happens, often years later when the company is worth more.

Automatic Debt Clearance: Once the debentures are converted into shares, the company no longer has to worry about paying back the principal amount or the interest, as the debt simply disappears and becomes equity.

Limitations of the Issue of Preference Shares / Convertible Debentures

While these instruments are excellent for flexibility, they carry specific "hidden" costs and risks that can complicate a company's financial health.

Limitations of Preference Shares

Fixed Financial Burden: Even though dividends aren't always legally mandatory, they are usually cumulative. This means if the company skips a payment during a bad year, the debt to shareholders piles up and must be cleared before any common equity holders get a cent.

High Cost of Capital: Because preference shares are riskier for investors than debt (no collateral), investors demand a higher dividend rate. Unlike interest on a loan, these dividends are not tax-deductible, making them more expensive for the company than a bank loan.

Impact on Equity Value: Since preference shareholders have a "priority claim" on profits and assets, having too many of them can make the common stock less attractive to major institutional investors.

Limitations of Convertible Debentures

The Dilution "Time Bomb": The biggest drawback is future dilution. When the debt converts, a massive influx of new shares hits the market. This dilutes the Earnings Per Share (EPS) and the voting power of existing shareholders.

Loss of Tax Shield: Once the debentures convert into equity, the company loses the ability to deduct interest payments from its taxable income, potentially increasing its tax bill.

Financial Distress Risk: Unlike preference shares, the interest on debentures is a legal obligation. If the company has a terrible year and can't pay the interest, it can be forced into bankruptcy by the debenture holders.

Conversion Uncertainty: If the company’s stock price performs poorly and stays below the "conversion price," investors may choose not to convert. The company is then stuck having to pay back the full principal amount in cash, which can cause a sudden liquidity crisis.

Eligible Criteria for Issue of Preference Shares / Convertible Debentures

To issue Preference Shares or Convertible Debentures in India, a company must adhere to specific legal frameworks—primarily Section 55 (for Preference Shares) and Section 71 (for Debentures) of the Companies Act, 2013, along with relevant SEBI regulations for listed entities.

Eligible Criteria for Preference Shares

A company cannot simply issue preference shares; it must meet these "pre-flight" requirements:

Articles of Association (AoA) Authorization: The company’s AoA must contain a specific clause authorizing the issuance of preference shares. If absent, the AoA must be amended first.

No Subsisting Defaults: At the time of issue, the company must not have any existing defaults in:

Redeeming previously issued preference shares.

Paying dividends due on any existing preference shares.

Special Resolution (SR): The issue must be approved by the shareholders via a Special Resolution (requiring at least 75% majority vote) in a General Meeting.

Redemption Limit (The 20-Year Rule): * Preference shares must be redeemable. Irredeemable preference shares are prohibited in India.

The maximum tenure is 20 years.

Exception: Companies in Infrastructure Projects can issue them for up to 30 years, provided they begin redeeming 10% of the shares annually from the 21st year.

Eligible Criteria for Convertible Debentures

Debentures that convert into equity are governed by stricter rules to prevent sudden, unauthorized dilution of ownership:

Special Resolution (SR) Requirement: According to Section 71(1), a company can issue debentures with an option to convert them into shares only if a Special Resolution is passed by the shareholders.

No Voting Rights: Debentures (whether convertible or not) cannot carry any voting rights.

Credit Rating (For Public/Rights Issues): Listed companies must obtain a credit rating from at least one recognized agency before issuing convertible debt to the public.

Debenture Trustee: If the debentures have a maturity exceeding 18 months, the company must appoint a Debenture Trustee to protect the interests of the holders.

Debenture Redemption Reserve (DRR): Companies must create a DRR out of their profits to ensure there is enough money to pay back the non-convertible portion of the debt, if any.

Documents Required for Issue of Preference Shares / Convertible Debentures

When a company issues Preference Shares or Convertible Debentures, the documentation is designed to track the transition from a "proposal" to "actual ownership/debt." In India, these are primarily governed by the Companies Act, 2013 and the PAS (Prospectus and Allotment of Securities) rules.

The process is generally divided into Authorization, Offer, and Allotment stages.

Authorization & Approval Documents

Before reaching out to investors, the company must prove it has the "right" to issue these securities.

Updated Articles of Association (AoA): Must contain the power to issue preference shares or debentures.

Board Resolution: A certified copy of the minutes where the Board approved the issue, the price, and the terms (e.g., dividend rate or interest rate).

Special Resolution (SR): Required for both preference shares and convertible debt. This is filed with the ROC in Form MGT-14 within 30 days.

Explanatory Statement: A detailed note attached to the meeting notice explaining the "why," the "how much," and the "at what price" to the shareholders.

The "Offer" Phase (Private Placement)

If the company is offering these to a select group of people (Private Placement), specific forms are mandatory:

Form PAS-4 (Letter of Offer): This is the official invitation sent to identified investors. It contains the company’s financial health and the terms of the security.

Form PAS-5 (Record of Private Placement): An internal record maintained by the company listing everyone to whom the offer was sent.

Valuation Report: A report from a Registered Valuer justifying the price of the shares or the conversion price of the debentures.

The "Allotment" Phase (Final Filings)

Once investors pay and shares/debentures are assigned:

Form PAS-3 (Return of Allotment): Filed with the ROC within 15–30 days of allotment. It includes a complete "List of Allottees" (names, addresses, and amount invested).

Share Certificates (Form SH-1): Issued to preference shareholders within 2 months.

Debenture Certificates: Issued to debenture holders within 6 months.

Debenture Trust Deed (Form SH-12): If issuing secured debentures, this legal contract between the company and the Debenture Trustee is mandatory.

Fees for Issue of Preference Shares / Convertible Debentures in India

Raising capital through Preference Shares or Convertible Debentures involves three types of costs: government filing fees, statutory taxes (Stamp Duty), and professional service fees.

ROC Filing Fees (Government Fees)

For every stage of the issue, the company must file specific e-forms with the Registrar of Companies (ROC). The fees are tiered based on the company's Authorized Share Capital:


| Form No. | Purpose | Normal Fee (for Capital > Rs.1 Cr) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| MGT-14 | Filing the Special Resolution | Rs.600 |
| PAS-3 | Return of Allotment (Final Report) | Rs.600 |
| SH-7 | If you need to increase Authorized Capital first | Varies by capital amount |


Note: If you miss the 30-day deadline, "Additional Fees" kick in, which can be as high as 12 times the normal fee.

Stamp Duty (The Mandatory Tax)

Stamp duty is a significant cost and is now largely uniform across India for securities. As per the Indian Stamp Act (amended):

Issue of Preference Shares: 0.005% of the total issue value (including the premium).

Issue of Debentures: 0.005% of the total face value of the debentures.

Transfer of Securities: If these are later traded, a duty of 0.015% (for delivery-based) or 0.0001% (for debenture transfers) applies.

Professional & Intermediary Fees

For a formal issue, especially in listed companies or large private placements, you will incur these professional costs:

Merchant Banker Fees: For listed companies, appointing a Merchant Banker is mandatory. Their fees vary based on the issue size but typically start from several lakhs.

Valuation Report Fees: A Registered Valuer must certify the price of the shares or the conversion ratio of the debentures. Their fee usually ranges from ₹20,000 to ₹1,00,000+ depending on complexity.

Credit Rating Fees: If issuing Convertible Debentures to the public, you must get a rating from agencies like CRISIL or ICRA. They typically charge around 0.1% of the total issue size.

Debenture Trustee Fees: If a trustee is required, they charge an annual fee for protecting the interests of the debenture holders.
